# A practical sight-reading curriculum

## Strategy

Use a graded sequence of easy, unfamiliar music alongside your repertoire. Keep K545 and Rondo alla Turca in the repertoire half of the session. They are useful for enjoyment, control, phrasing and focused technical work, but their memorized difficulty does not tell us your current reading level.

The aim is to make the connection **notation → musical pattern → keyboard movement** increasingly direct, while keeping time. Note naming alone is only one component. Rhythm, coordination, keyboard geography, harmony and listening all need attention.

This is an AI-organized practice plan using existing educational material, not an accredited course or an assessment of your playing. The free score library supplies repertoire at broad difficulty levels. A published graded sight-reading book supplies a tighter sequence if you want one. AI is useful for adapting the plan to a log; a teacher can hear and see problems this page cannot.

Your earlier lessons do not establish whether teaching was ineffective. Inconsistent practice could have contributed, but the lesson design, level and feedback are unknown. A useful future lesson would begin with an unseen excerpt and end with two specific practice targets. A diagnostic lesson now, followed by a check every two to four weeks if affordable, is my suggested way to get feedback without making lessons the whole plan.

## First session: find your starting point

Use the first 30 minutes for placement, then enjoy your normal repertoire block.

1. **Five minutes: map notation to the keyboard.** In [Note Identification](https://www.musictheory.net/exercises/note), use Customize (gear) to choose treble clef, C major/no accidentals and C4–G4. Say and play each note. Then try bass clef C3–G3. C4 is middle C. Widen the range if this is automatic; narrow it if you are counting every staff line.
2. **Five minutes: test the pulse.** Open the [simple rhythm preset](https://sightread.org/?rhythm-reading#t=60&m=4&ts=44&up=q,h,w&ur=0.1&c=yes). Count and tap it before listening to playback. A quarter note lasts one beat, a half note two and a whole note four in 4/4; a rest occupies time silently. Choose another exercise for each first attempt.
3. **Fifteen minutes: test short scores.** Try 4–8 bars of [Les Cloches](https://www.gmajormusictheory.org/Freebies/Primer/pLesCloches/pLesCloches.pdf). If you repeatedly stop, try a standard-staff [First Pieces](https://www.gmajormusictheory.org/Freebies/freebiesFP.html) excerpt or one hand. If comfortable, sample a different [Primer](https://www.gmajormusictheory.org/Freebies/freebiesP.html) score, then a [Level 1](https://www.gmajormusictheory.org/Freebies/freebies1.html) excerpt. Use new excerpts for each test. The drill's narrow note range is a starting exercise, not a guarantee that every library score uses only those notes.
4. **Five minutes: record a baseline.** Write the score title/link, bars, hands, tempo, whether the pulse continued, and rough pitch/rhythm accuracy. Record audio on your phone if useful. Choose the easiest stage that addresses a real weakness; do not spend two weeks on something already fluent.

Try quarter note = 40–60 BPM in simple meter, but choose the tempo from the hardest bar. A slower tempo is acceptable. If even a very slow beat is impossible to sustain, simplify the music instead of stretching every beat indefinitely.

## How to use each new excerpt

**Preview for 30–60 seconds.** Check clefs, key signature, time signature, starting notes and fingerings. Notice repeated shapes and one likely difficulty. Locate your hands and count one bar in. For a comparable benchmark, keep the preview duration consistent and do not rehearse the excerpt.

**Play once, continuously.** Let a wrong note go and recover at the next beat. Read a little ahead when you can; there is no fixed number of bars you must look ahead. For your first weeks, avoid pedal so it is easier to hear durations and coordination. Use brief keyboard glances for real shifts rather than staring down after every note.

**Then check and repair.** Compare the score with your recording, or listen to the source audio only after the first attempt. You may need to play slowly again to locate the mistake. Repair one short passage and move to fresh music. Replaying is valuable practice, but it is no longer a first-sight attempt. Do not use a tutorial or fingering video before your benchmark.

Keep both kinds of practice: continuity during the first pass, and accuracy during the repair pass. “Keep going” does not mean ignoring the same mistake every day.

## Six stages

The suggested weeks are review points. Stay longer if needed; skip a stage only after fresh examples show it is comfortable. Library levels below are the website's repertoire categories, **not ABRSM grades**, and individual pieces vary.

| Stage | Suggested review window | Main challenge | Material |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Weeks 1–2 | Steps, thirds, both clefs, quarters/halves/whole notes; 4–8 bars | Standard-staff First Pieces, then easy Primer |
| 2 | Weeks 3–4 | Both staves; hands alternating or a melody over held bass; 3/4; 4–8 bars | Primer |
| 3 | Weeks 5–6 | G then F major; paired eighths; small shifts; 8 bars | Primer → Level 1 |
| 4 | Weeks 7–8 | Chord shapes and repeated accompaniment; ties or dotted rhythms; 8–12 bars | Level 1 |
| 5 | Weeks 9–10 | Longer phrases, line changes and expression; optional D/B-flat or simple 6/8; 8–16 bars | Level 1 → Level 2 |
| 6 | Weeks 11–12 onward | Mixed familiar keys/textures, broader easy repertoire and regular benchmarks | Highest level that stays fluent |

The [practice page](https://kiankyars.github.io/piano/) gives each stage's note targets, rhythm link, score library, technique task and advancement criterion. Use it at the piano; you need not reread this document daily.

For 6/8, feel two dotted-quarter beats, each split into three eighth notes. Begin by tapping the pattern away from the keyboard. The default stage links stay in simple meter; use [this separate 6/8 drill](https://sightread.org/?rhythm-reading#t=60&m=4&ts=68&up=cq.,ceee,cqe,ceq&ur=0&c=yes) only when ready. Check how its playback marks subdivisions before using it as a tempo reference. Do not add an unfamiliar key and meter in the same reading.

## When to advance, repeat or simplify

Use this **practical heuristic**, not an official exam standard:

- Advance after **three fresh excerpts on different days** at the current demands with a continuous pulse, no restarts and roughly 90% correct pitches **and** durations. Include both clefs, and hands together when that is the stage's target.
- Keep conditions comparable: log the tempo and beat unit, bars, key, hands and texture. A familiar repeat or much easier piece cannot establish improvement at a harder level.
- Change **one variable**: range, rhythm, key, coordination or tempo. You need not raise the tempo whenever you advance.
- Repeated stops, losing your place or many guessed notes: reduce the range/length, use simpler rhythms, or choose a lower-level score. If playing separately, return to a simpler two-hand excerpt as soon as possible.
- Good pitch reading but poor rhythm: retain the level of pitch difficulty and simplify rhythm. Good rhythm but bass-clef hesitation: put more of the short mapping drill into bass clef.

Accuracy is an estimate, not an automatic score. If counting helps, roughly 18 correct note events out of 20 is 90%; assess rhythm separately, including rests and held notes. Record “uncertain” instead of manufacturing precision. Pulse and accuracy both matter. Do not fill the log while playing.

Every seventh session, record a **new, unfamiliar** excerpt at the same difficulty and tempo as your previous benchmark. Compare interruptions, pitch mistakes and duration mistakes. At four-week reviews, look for fewer stops, more fluent unfamiliar bars or broader comfortable material, not just faster performances of memorized pieces. If there is no change, check difficulty, practice consistency and feedback before adding minutes.

## Your repertoire half-hour

**5 minutes: technique in context.** Use a five-finger pattern, scale, chord shape or shift from today's music. Play comfortably and listen for evenness. Start hands separately if necessary. This is about coordinated movement, not speed contests or maximum repetitions. Stop if playing hurts; seek hands-on feedback for recurring tension.

**20 minutes: build a piece.** Choose one observable goal, such as an even left-hand figure or a clear melody over soft accompaniment. Work on one or two short passages, fix fingering, then connect them to their surrounding phrases. Read the score even when you remember the notes. Alternate sessions between your Mozart pieces and a much easier new piece learned from notation. You can use a performance recording after your own reading to study interpretation.

**5 minutes: play for enjoyment.** Play something familiar freely. Memorization remains useful and welcome here. Do not let entire-piece run-throughs consume the focused practice block.

## Consistency and the long term

Attach practice to an existing daily cue. Set the score and next excerpt out before stopping. The session selector counts practice sessions, not calendar days; missing a day creates no catch-up debt.

On a busy day, do **10 minutes**: 2 minutes rhythm, 6 minutes fresh easy reading, 2 minutes checking/logging. This preserves the habit; it is not equivalent to a full session. Take a brief break between halves of the hour when useful. The seventh session can be a benchmark plus relaxed playing rather than another intensive hour.

After the initial review cycle, retain 15–20 minutes of fresh reading on most practice days. Gradually reduce the isolated note drill as it becomes easy and spend those minutes reading real music. Expand into easy classical miniatures, varied accompaniments and, when available, simple duets. Keep the music easy enough to preserve flow and musical attention.

If you adopt the optional Paul Harris book, work through its rhythm/melody preparation and prepared pieces in order, then use its fresh tests as directed. Use this within the same 30-minute reading budget. Supplement with new library scores; do not reread the same test and treat it as new evidence. Move from Initial to Grade 1 and onward by fluency, not by the original 12-week schedule.

No plan can promise a particular sight-reading level in a fixed number of hours. The goal is a measurable change in what you can read **the first time**, while keeping playing enjoyable enough to continue.
